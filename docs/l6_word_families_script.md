# L6 · Word Families — script

**One video, not three.** Level 5 was six separate rules, which is why it split. Level 6 is
ONE mechanism repeated thirteen times: change the first letter, keep the ending. Splitting
that would break the only thing that makes it powerful — watching it repeat until the child
realises they can do it without help.

One continuous take, same teacher. Target ≈ **9:30**.

---

## Source of truth — the app's own module

`iOS/Learn English/UI/Phonics/6 Word Families/`. Thirteen families, eighty-one words, and
the word lists below are the app's exactly. The app's own wording is used where it is good:

* intro — *"Change the first letter to build new words"*
* learn — *"All these words end in ‑at"* · *"Tap a letter to build a word"*
* practice — *"Find the First Letter — which letter starts this word?"*

| family | words |
|---|---|
| ‑at | cat bat hat rat mat sat pat fat |
| ‑an | can man fan ran pan tan van ban |
| ‑ap | cap map nap tap lap gap |
| ‑en | hen ten pen men den |
| ‑ig | big pig dig wig jig fig |
| ‑it | sit bit hit fit kit pit wit |
| ‑in | pin win fin tin bin |
| ‑og | dog log fog hog jog |
| ‑ot | hot pot dot lot cot rot got |
| ‑op | top hop mop pop cop |
| ‑un | sun run fun bun gun pun |
| ‑ug | bug rug hug mug dug jug |
| ‑all | ball tall wall fall call hall mall |

**Ordered by vowel, not by the app's menu order.** The app lists ‑at, ‑an, ‑og, ‑en… which
jumps between vowels. Grouping a‑e‑i‑o‑u reuses the short-vowel spine from Level 2, so the
child hears the vowel HOLD STILL while only the front letter moves. That contrast is the
whole lesson and it is lost if the vowel changes every family.

## Two things the script must not get wrong

1. **‑all is not one of the other twelve, and its vowel is never named.** `ball` is not
   `b` + short‑a + `ll`. The vowel is /ɔː/ — the sound in *saw* and *call* — which is NOT
   the /ɒ/ of *hot* that the app's own chart gives for the letter o. An earlier draft said
   it "says /or/", which is worse still: in a rhotic accent "or" carries an audible r and
   `ball` has none. So the script names no symbol at all and teaches ‑all as a CHUNK —
   three letters that say "all" — which is how it is actually read. It also earns a
   callback: two l's, which is the Floss rule from Level 5.
2. **Never say "onset" or "rime" to the child.** The app says *"change the first letter"*
   and so does the script. The jargon appears once, for the parent, and never again.

## Voice rules (carried from L5)

Never more than two statements without a *look*, *listen* or *you*. Praise after every
group, not once at the end. Don't answer every question instantly. Whole sentences.

# THE SCRIPT

## 1 · WELCOME (0:00 – 0:50)

Welcome back!

Last time we finished Level Five. You learned six spelling rules, and you learned them all.

Today we are starting Level Six, and this one is different. Today you are not learning a
rule. Today you are learning a trick — a trick that lets you read a whole pile of words at
once.

Let me show you what I mean.

Here is a word. Cat.

Now watch what happens when I change just ONE letter. The first one.

Cat… bat.

Did you see that? Everything else stayed the same. Only the front changed, and it made a
brand new word.

That is the trick. And once you know it, you can read hundreds of words.

## 2 · THE FIRST FAMILY · ‑at (0:50 – 2:00)

Look at the end of the word. C‑A‑T. The last two letters are a and t.

Say that ending with me. ‑at.

Now here is the important part. Every word that ends in those two letters says ‑at. So all I
have to do is put a different letter in front, and I get another word I can read.

Watch. I will keep ‑at and change only the front.

Cat. Bat. Hat. Rat. Mat. Sat. Pat. Fat.

Eight words. And you only had to learn one ending.

That is called a word family. All those words are in the ‑at family, because they all end
the same way.

You just read eight words. Well done.

## 3 · MORE a FAMILIES · ‑an and ‑ap (2:00 – 3:10)

Let's keep the same vowel and change the ending.

Here is our next family. ‑an.

Listen to the vowel. It is still a short a, just like ‑at. Only the last letter changed.

Can. Man. Fan. Ran. Pan. Tan. Van. Ban.

Eight more. That is sixteen words already.

And one more a family. ‑ap.

Cap. Map. Nap. Tap. Lap. Gap.

Look at what is happening. The a stays put every single time. It is only the letters around
it that move.

You are doing really well.

## 4 · THE e FAMILY · ‑en (3:10 – 3:45)

Now we change the vowel. This time it is a short e.

Our family is ‑en.

Hen. Ten. Pen. Men. Den.

Say the ending with me. ‑en.

Five words, and the same trick worked again.

## 5 · THE i FAMILIES · ‑ig, ‑it, ‑in (3:45 – 5:00)

Now a short i. We have three families here, so listen carefully to the ending each time.

First, ‑ig.

Big. Pig. Dig. Wig. Jig. Fig.

Next, ‑it.

Sit. Bit. Hit. Fit. Kit. Pit. Wit.

And ‑in.

Pin. Win. Fin. Tin. Bin.

Did you notice something? All three of those families have the same vowel. The i sound never
changed. Only the letter at the end did.

That is eighteen more words. You are reading beautifully.

## 6 · THE o FAMILIES · ‑og, ‑ot, ‑op (5:00 – 6:10)

Now a short o. Three families again.

‑og. Dog. Log. Fog. Hog. Jog.

‑ot. Hot. Pot. Dot. Lot. Cot. Rot. Got.

‑op. Top. Hop. Mop. Pop. Cop.

Listen to the middle of every one of those words. It is the same o sound every time.

## 7 · THE u FAMILIES · ‑un and ‑ug (6:10 – 7:00)

Our last short vowel is u.

‑un. Sun. Run. Fun. Bun. Gun. Pun.

‑ug. Bug. Rug. Hug. Mug. Dug. Jug.

That is twelve more words from two endings.

## 8 · A DIFFERENT KIND OF FAMILY · ‑all (7:00 – 7:50)

Now I want to show you one more family, but I have to be honest with you about it, because
it does not work quite the same way.

Here it is. ‑all.

Ball. Tall. Wall. Fall. Call. Hall. Mall.

The trick still works. Change the front letter, get a new word. But listen to the middle.

Ball.

That is not a short a. It does not say /a/ like cat. It has changed into something else.

So we do not sound this family out letter by letter. We learn the whole ending in one
piece. When you see those three letters together — a, l, l — you say "all".

Ball. Tall. Wall. Say the ending with me. ‑all.

And look at the ending. Two l's. Do you remember why? That is the Floss rule from Level
Five — one clap, short vowel, ends in l, so we double it.

## 9 · YOUR TURN (7:50 – 8:50)

Now it is your turn, and this is exactly what the app will ask you.

I am going to show you a word, and you tell me which letter goes at the front.

Here is the ending. ‑en. And here is the picture. A hen.

Which letter starts it?

*(pause ≈ 2.5 seconds)*

H. Hen. That is right.

Let's do one more. The ending is ‑ug. And the picture is a bug.

Which letter starts it?

*(pause ≈ 2.5 seconds)*

B. Bug. Excellent.

That is the whole skill. Find the ending you know, then work out the front.

## 10 · LET'S REMEMBER (8:50 – 9:15)

Let's say what we learned today.

A word family is a group of words that all end the same way.

If you can read the ending, you can read every word in the family. You only have to change
the letter at the front.

Today you met thirteen families, and you read eighty-one words.

Eighty-one. From thirteen endings. That is what makes this trick so useful.

## 11 · CLOSE (9:15 – 9:35)

I am really proud of you. This is the skill that turns sounding out into reading.

Next time we will keep going with Level Six and practise these families until they are easy.

Don't miss it!

If you enjoyed this lesson, please like this video and subscribe.

And practise every one of these families in the English Learning app. It's free on the Apple
App Store and Google Play.

See you next time!

## A note on the quiz words

`cat` is deliberately NOT used here. It already carries L4 CVC, Read Your First Sentences
and Letter Recognition, so by Level 6 a child has met it in four videos and it no longer
tests anything. `hen` and `bug` are fresh, both draw clearly at thumbnail size, and their
answers are different letters — H and B — so the second question cannot be guessed from
the first.

## What the take actually transcribed as (measured 2026-08-10)

The recording is ONE continuous take and is complete — all 81 words are spoken. But the
transcript is not usable as-is, and the reason is specific:

**Whisper substitutes real words for spoken phonics fragments.** It has no way to write an
ending said in isolation, so it reaches for the nearest dictionary word:

| spoken | transcribed as |
|---|---|
| ‑in (the ending) | "An." |
| ‑un (the ending) | "An." |
| in | "Inn." |
| fin | "Finn." |
| pun | "Pan." |

It also RUNS FAMILIES TOGETHER on one line — `Pit. Wit. An. Inn. Pin. Win. Finn.` merges the
tail of ‑it into the whole of ‑in — which is why `tin` and `bin` appear to be missing when
they are not.

Script-to-take word match is 69%, so the teacher rephrased while speaking. Captions must be
built from the TAKE, never from this script, or the words on screen will not match the voice.

**Correction method:** the take follows the script's family order exactly, so each run can be
mapped onto its known word list from the table above rather than guessed at. Correct the text
first, then align, then snap.

## Build notes (after the recording exists)

1. `check_take_matches_script.py` **before** building. Forced alignment stamps the SCRIPT
   onto the audio, so a take that says something else still aligns cleanly. L5 Part 2's take
   matched only 73%.
2. `tools/align_audio.py --model small`. **Never `base`** — it silently dropped 27 seconds
   of Part 2, proven by 27 speech onsets inside a window transcribed as silence.
3. Whisper cannot write a phoneme. Every demonstration of a sound will come back as a real
   word or a hum and must be corrected by hand against the audio — `/ɔːl/` here.
4. `tools/snap_word_phrases.py` on every one-word run. This script has thirteen of them,
   which is the most of any lesson so far.
5. Audio length comes from the MP3, never from the transcript.
6. A new world — every video wears its own, and L5 used the Building Site, the Bell Tower
   and the Magic Stage. Candidate: **The Family House** — one house per family, the ending
   painted on the door, and the front letter swapping on the doorstep to make each new word.
